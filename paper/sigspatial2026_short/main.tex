\begin{abstract}
Cross-view disaster assessment combines ground-level and overhead imagery to
infer building damage, but aggregate F1 hides examples where the two views
disagree. This short paper introduces Conflict-Aware Evaluation (CAE), a
reporting protocol that measures overall F1, conflict-subset F1, cross-view gain
over the stronger single-view baseline, and the direction in which one view
corrects the other. We apply CAE to an Eaton/Altadena wildfire dataset with
property-centered ground imagery and to the Hurricane Ian CVDisaster benchmark
with panoramic ground imagery. The main result is deliberately mixed. On the
wildfire sensitivity split, cross-view fusion improves conflict F1 by 0.0388.
On the leakage-controlled hurricane endpoint split, the gain becomes negative
($\dview=-0.0153$), although aggregate F1 makes cross-view and street-only
models look nearly tied. Directional and alignment diagnostics show that fusion
helps when the ground view observes the target building, but can lose value when
the ground view becomes panoramic context. CAE therefore turns paired-view
triage into a stress test for when fusion helps, fails, or should defer.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{disaster assessment, cross-view learning, multimodal fusion, GeoAI, conflict-aware evaluation}

\section{Introduction}

Post-disaster building assessment increasingly uses several visual viewpoints.
Overhead imagery gives broad coverage, while ground imagery can expose facade
condition, debris, and local structural cues. Cross-view models seek to combine
these signals, but average test scores hide the cases that matter most for
deployment: buildings where the views disagree.

This paper treats paired-view triage as conflict resolution. Each example has a
ground image, an overhead patch, and a binary damage label. We ask whether
fusion improves the subset where the single-view predictors disagree, rather
than whether it only improves average F1. This framing matters because a
property inspection photograph and a panoramic street scene are both ground
views, yet they observe the labeled building in different ways.

The work builds on overhead damage benchmarks \cite{gupta2019creating}, fusion
models for disaster assessment \cite{xiao2023cross}, ground-view damage studies
\cite{zhai2020damage,xue2024post,yang2025hyperlocal}, and the paired Hurricane
Ian CVDisaster benchmark \cite{li2025cross}. CAE is related to selective and
disagreement-aware evaluation \cite{geifman2017selective}, but its reporting
unit is spatial: the disagreement subset, the single-view correction direction,
and the gain or loss from fusion under a given observation regime. The paper
makes three claims: CAE exposes conflict behavior, leakage-controlled hurricane
results set a negative boundary for fusion, and spatial observation regime helps
explain the dominant-view switch.

\subsection{Paired-View Triage Settings}

Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i^{(s)},x_i^{(r)},y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a paired disaster
triage dataset with a ground image, an overhead patch, and a binary label. We
compare street-only (S), remote-only (R), and cross-view (CV) predictors. CV
uses late fusion of ResNet18 features \cite{he2016deep}: both embeddings, their
absolute difference, and their elementwise product.

Table~\ref{tab:settings} summarizes the four evaluation settings. The wildfire
setting uses 19,720 usable Eaton/Altadena paired records derived from
property-level inspection photographs and matched overhead crops. The source
index retains object identifiers, coordinates, crop boxes, overhead tile IDs,
and coverage metadata, enabling object-level grouping and tile-level spatial
aggregation. The positive class is \texttt{Affected + Minor + Major +
Destroyed}. The hurricane setting uses the Hurricane Ian CVDisaster benchmark
\cite{li2025cross}, with 4,121 paired samples across minor, moderate, and severe
damage. We evaluate both the endpoint task (minor versus severe) and a broader
moderate-plus-severe task. The comparison is not a causal test of wildfire
versus hurricane; it contrasts property-centric and panoramic observation
regimes.

\subsection{Conflict-Aware Evaluation}

For each example, let $p_i^{(s)}$ and $p_i^{(r)}$ denote the positive-class
probabilities predicted by the street-only and remote-only models. CAE defines
the conflict subset
\begin{equation}
\conf=\{i: |p_i^{(s)}-p_i^{(r)}|>\tau\},
\end{equation}
with $\tau=0.1$ in the main analysis. For each model $m$, CAE reports standard
overall F1 and conflict F1 on $\conf$. It then computes the cross-view conflict
gain
\begin{equation}
\dview =
\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{crossview};\conf)
-
\max\{\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{street};\conf),
\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{remote};\conf)\}.
\end{equation}
The compact CAE report for each model is therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_{\tau}(m)=
\left(\mathrm{F1}(m;\mathcal{D}),\,
\mathrm{F1}(m;\conf),\, n_c/N\right),
\quad n_c=|\conf|.
\end{equation}
We also decompose conflicts by correction direction. Let
$\hat{y}_i^{(s)}=\mathbf{1}\{p_i^{(s)}\ge0.5\}$ and define
$\hat{y}_i^{(r)}$ analogously. With
$c_i^{(v)}=\mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}_i^{(v)}=y_i\}$, the two arbitration rates are
\begin{align}
\rho_{S{\rightarrow}O} &=
\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i\in\conf}c_i^{(s)}(1-c_i^{(r)}),\\
\rho_{O{\rightarrow}S} &=
\frac{1}{n_c}\sum_{i\in\conf}c_i^{(r)}(1-c_i^{(s)}).
\end{align}
$S{\rightarrow}O$ denotes street-correct and remote-wrong cases, and
$O{\rightarrow}S$ denotes the reverse. Because a prior hurricane endpoint split
had object-level leakage, the grouped-clean endpoint split is the conservative
control.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

Images are resized to 224 by 224 pixels and normalized for ImageNet-pretrained
ResNet18 encoders. Each view embedding is projected to 256 dimensions. Models
use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ for 10 epochs, and the best-validation-F1
checkpoint is evaluated on the held-out test split.

For reproducibility, the wildfire release is organized around split manifests
and a data-card summary rather than raw-image redistribution. The manifests keep
object IDs, image-source fields, coordinates, matched overhead tile names,
binary labels, and filtering flags. This makes the grouped split auditable and
lets future work reconstruct the paired-view benchmark from the original
imagery sources when access permits.

Figure~\ref{fig:result-diagnostics} explains why the mixed gains are useful
rather than inconvenient. In wildfire, $S{\rightarrow}O$ conflicts are more
common than $O{\rightarrow}S$ conflicts, so property-centered ground imagery is
the stronger arbitrator. In the broader hurricane split, the ordering reverses,
which is consistent with panoramic views that contain more scene context and
weaker target alignment. The threshold sweep and alignment diagnostics show
that the CAE pattern is not only a single-threshold artifact.

Additional checks support the same ordering without changing the claim. At
$\tau=\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$, CV remains strongest on wildfire conflict accuracy
(0.8349, 0.7821, 0.7538). At $\tau=0.1$, wildfire conflict accuracy is 0.7885
for CV, 0.7356 for S, and 0.6529 for R; the hurricane moderate-plus-severe split
gives 0.7500, 0.7031, and 0.7266. Multi-seed validation also keeps CV slightly
ahead in base F1, while generic CLIP and DINOv2 features underperform the
supervised CNN baselines in this disaster-specific setting.

The tile-level panel adds a geographic check on this interpretation. Wildfire
damage, disagreement density, and cross-view gain are spatially uneven across
overhead tiles. High damage rate does not automatically imply high conflict,
and high conflict does not automatically imply positive CV gain. This matters
for disaster response because deployment errors are rarely spread uniformly
over a region. A model can look strong on a pooled metric while failing in a
cluster of parcels with unusual viewing angle, smoke shadow, vegetation cover,
or partial structure visibility. CAE therefore works as both a model-comparison
metric and a spatial audit layer: it identifies where the disagreement cases
are, which view wins there, and whether the fused model resolves or amplifies
the local ambiguity.

\section{Discussion}

The grouped-clean hurricane result is the key stress test: more visual evidence
does not always improve decisions once object-level leakage is controlled. CAE
therefore exposes a deployment boundary, not only a performance gain.

The conflict subset is not meant to replace the full test set. It is a second
readout that asks a narrower deployment question: when the two available views
would support different decisions, does fusion add useful evidence or blur the
better view? This differs from selecting examples by loss or label difficulty
after evaluating CV. The gate is defined only by the two single-view probability
outputs, so it can be computed before choosing whether to trust fusion. The
directional counts then make the error source visible. If most conflicts are
$S{\rightarrow}O$, the street view supplies the missing local cue; if most are
$O{\rightarrow}S$, the overhead view is the stronger arbitrator. A useful
paired-view benchmark should report both cases because a positive average gain
can hide a failure on the examples where fusion is expected to help.

Spatial observation regime makes this boundary plausible. In the wildfire
records, the photographer usually stands near the parcel and aims at the
structure that receives the label. The ground image can therefore carry direct
evidence about charred walls, damaged openings, debris against the facade, or
an intact exterior. In the hurricane records, the panoramic image often captures
the street segment rather than a single labeled footprint. It may show flooded
roads, vegetation loss, roof debris, and neighboring buildings, but the target
building can be small, peripheral, or occluded. The mechanism is therefore how
each view observes the target, not simply ``street versus satellite.'' The same
architecture sees two views in both settings, but the semantic access of each
view differs.

Operationally, cases where S and R agree may use the agreement decision; cases
where they disagree should route to CV or human review. Future datasets should
collect property-centered and panoramic views for the same target buildings to
test target alignment directly.

The threshold $\tau$ should be interpreted as an operating knob rather than a
fixed scientific constant. A small threshold sends more examples into the
conflict set and measures broad disagreement. A larger threshold keeps only the
strongest single-view contradictions, which may be more suitable when human
review capacity is limited. The main analysis uses $\tau=0.1$ because it leaves
enough examples for stable reporting while still isolating visible disagreement.
The threshold sweep in Figure~\ref{fig:result-diagnostics} is therefore part of
the claim. It shows whether the conclusion survives when the triage system
keeps fewer but harder cases.

The study still has limits. The wildfire and hurricane settings differ in
hazard type, geography, collection protocol, and label construction, so the
paper treats observation regime as an explanatory lens rather than a causal
proof. The compact image-alignment proxies in Figure~\ref{fig:result-diagnostics}
are diagnostic, not a replacement for field-verified viewpoint geometry. A
stronger benchmark would collect several ground-view regimes for the same
buildings and report CAE under identical spatial splits.

Two implementation details also deserve care in future work. First, the fusion
model is deliberately simple, so the negative grouped-clean hurricane result
should not be read as a universal limit of cross-view learning. It shows that
late fusion can fail to improve the hardest cases after leakage control. Second,
object grouping matters as much as architecture. If visually similar views of
the same object appear across splits, a model can appear to learn cross-view
reasoning while partly memorizing object-specific cues. CAE is most useful when
paired with grouped splits, transparent manifests, and geographic diagnostics.
Those controls turn fusion from a single leaderboard number into an auditable
decision rule.

\section{Conclusion}

CAE reframes paired-view disaster triage as a conflict-resolution problem. It
shows a positive wildfire conflict gain, a negative grouped-clean hurricane
gain, and a dominant-view switch tied to spatial observation regime. Disaster
benchmarks should therefore report conflict-subset behavior, directional
arbitration, and threshold sensitivity alongside aggregate scores. This added
reporting does not require a new model. It changes what the evaluation asks:
whether fusion helps exactly where single-view evidence disagrees.

The recommended reporting template is compact. For each split, authors should
publish full-test F1, conflict F1 for each view and the fused model, the number
of conflict cases, the cross-view gain over the best single view, and the
$S{\rightarrow}O$ versus $O{\rightarrow}S$ correction balance. When possible,
these numbers should be paired with grouped splits and a small spatial summary
of where conflicts occur. This template would make negative results useful
rather than embarrassing. If fusion loses on the conflict subset, the benchmark
has revealed a boundary condition. If fusion wins, the directional counts show
which view supplied the missing evidence. Either outcome gives disaster
analysts a more actionable evaluation than a single average score.

For this reason, the contribution is evaluative rather than architectural. The
paper does not argue that one fusion block is optimal, or that one disaster type
always favors one view. It argues that paired-view benchmarks need a sharper
unit of analysis. Conflict cases reveal the operational value of fusion because
they are the cases where two plausible sensors disagree. Reporting those cases
also makes future architectural work easier to judge: a stronger model should
not only improve pooled F1, but also reduce the unresolved conflict left by its
single-view components.
