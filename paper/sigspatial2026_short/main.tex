\begin{abstract}
Cross-view disaster assessment combines ground-level and overhead imagery to
infer building damage, but aggregate F1 hides the examples where the two views
disagree. This paper introduces Conflict-Aware Evaluation (CAE), a reporting
protocol that measures overall F1, conflict-subset F1, cross-view gain over the
stronger single-view baseline, and the direction in which one view corrects the
other. We apply CAE to an Eaton/Altadena wildfire dataset with property-centered
ground imagery and to the Hurricane Ian CVDisaster benchmark with panoramic
ground imagery. Results differ by observation regime. On the wildfire
sensitivity split, cross-view fusion improves conflict F1 by $\dview=+0.0388$,
and a label-independence permutation test rejects the null at $p<10^{-4}$. On
the hurricane endpoint task, the picture inverts once spatial leakage is
removed: the same task shows a $+0.0805$ conflict gain under an object-leaky
split but a slightly negative gain ($\dview=-0.0153$, within overlapping
bootstrap intervals) under an object-grouped clean split, even though aggregate
F1 makes cross-view and street-only models look nearly tied. Directional and
alignment diagnostics tie this to how the ground view sees the target: wildfire
conflict images contain roughly seventeen times more building pixels than
hurricane conflict images, and fusion loses value when the ground view becomes
panoramic context rather than a property-centered observation. CAE therefore
turns paired-view triage into a stress test for when fusion helps, fails, or
should defer.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{disaster assessment, cross-view, multimodal fusion, GeoAI, conflict-aware evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
Post-disaster building assessment increasingly uses several visual viewpoints.
Overhead imagery gives broad coverage, while ground imagery can expose facade
condition, debris, and local structural cues. Cross-view models seek to combine
these signals, building on a long line of cross-view geolocalization and
matching work that aligns ground and aerial observations of the same
place~\cite{workman2015wide}. Yet average test scores hide the cases that matter
most for deployment: the buildings where the views disagree.

This paper treats paired-view triage as conflict resolution. Each example has a
ground image, an overhead patch, and a binary damage label. We ask whether
fusion improves the subset where the single-view predictors disagree, rather
than whether it only improves average F1. This framing matters because a
property inspection photograph and a panoramic street scene are both ground
views, yet they observe the labeled building in very different ways.

The work builds on overhead damage benchmarks~\cite{gupta2019creating}, fusion
models for disaster assessment~\cite{xiao2023cross}, ground-view damage
studies~\cite{zhai2020damage,xue2024post,yang2025hyperlocal}, and the paired
Hurricane Ian CVDisaster benchmark~\cite{li2025cross}. CAE is related to
selective prediction~\cite{geifman2017selective} because both settings identify
cases where an automatic decision may need extra scrutiny, but CAE does not
estimate risk--coverage curves or abstention. Its reporting unit is spatial: the
disagreement subset, the single-view correction direction, and the gain or loss
from fusion under a given observation regime.

We make three contributions. (i) We define CAE, a compact protocol that reports
conflict-subset F1, the directional arbitration balance, and the cross-view
conflict gain $\dview$ alongside aggregate F1. (ii) Using CAE across two
disasters and four splits, we show that the value of fusion tracks the
\emph{spatial observation regime}---how a ground view aligns with the labeled
building---more closely than it tracks aggregate F1. (iii) We show that the most
widely reported reading of cross-view benefit can be an artifact of spatial
leakage: on a single hurricane endpoint task, controlling object-level leakage
flips the conflict gain from clearly positive to slightly negative, turning a
``cross-view helps'' result into a deployment boundary that CAE makes visible.

\subsection{Paired-View Triage Settings}

Let $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i^{(s)},x_i^{(r)},y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a paired disaster
triage dataset with a ground image, an overhead patch, and a binary label. We
compare street-only (S), remote-only (R), and cross-view (CV) predictors. CV
uses late fusion of ResNet18 features~\cite{he2016deep}: both embeddings, their
absolute difference, and their elementwise product. The three predictors share
the same backbone and training budget so that CAE compares evidence access, not
model capacity.

Table~\ref{tab:settings} summarizes the four evaluation settings. The wildfire
setting uses 19{,}720 usable Eaton/Altadena paired records derived from
property-level inspection photographs and matched overhead crops. The positive
class is \texttt{Affected + Minor + Major + Destroyed}. The hurricane setting
uses the Hurricane Ian CVDisaster benchmark~\cite{li2025cross}, with 4{,}121
paired samples across minor, moderate, and severe damage. We evaluate the
endpoint task (minor versus severe) under both a leaky and a leakage-controlled
split, and a broader moderate-plus-severe task. We use these settings to
contrast property-centric and panoramic observation regimes.

\paragraph{Wildfire data statement.}
The wildfire records derive from property-level post-fire inspection
photographs of the 2025 Eaton/Altadena fire, each matched to an overhead crop
of the same parcel. Every record retains an object identifier, parcel
coordinates, the crop box, the overhead tile ID, and coverage flags, so the
benchmark supports object-level grouping and tile-level spatial aggregation. We
use an object-grouped 80/10/10 train/validation/test split
($\approx$15.8k/2.0k/2.0k records) with approximate label stratification. To
avoid redistributing source imagery, the release is organized around split
manifests and a data card rather than raw images; the manifests keep object IDs,
image-source fields, coordinates, matched overhead tile names, binary labels,
and filtering flags, making the grouped split auditable when future work
reconstructs the benchmark from the original sources.

\subsection{Conflict-Aware Evaluation}

For each example, let $p_i^{(s)}$ and $p_i^{(r)}$ denote the positive-class
probabilities predicted by the street-only and remote-only models. CAE defines
the conflict subset
\begin{equation}
\conf=\{i: |p_i^{(s)}-p_i^{(r)}|>\tau\},
\end{equation}
with $\tau=0.1$ in the main analysis. For each model $m$, CAE reports standard
overall F1 and conflict F1 on $\conf$. It then computes the cross-view conflict
gain
\begin{equation}
\dview =
\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{crossview};\conf)
-
\max\{\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{street};\conf),
\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{remote};\conf)\}.
\end{equation}
The compact CAE report for each model is therefore
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_{\tau}(m)=
\left(\mathrm{F1}(m;\mathcal{D}),\,
\mathrm{F1}(m;\conf),\, |\conf|/N\right).
\end{equation}
We also decompose conflicts by correction direction. Let
$\hat{y}_i^{(s)}=\mathbf{1}\{p_i^{(s)}\ge0.5\}$ and define $\hat{y}_i^{(r)}$
analogously. With $c_i^{(v)}=\mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}_i^{(v)}=y_i\}$, the two
arbitration rates are
\begin{align}
\rho_{S{\rightarrow}O} &=
\frac{1}{|\conf|}\sum_{i\in\conf}c_i^{(s)}(1-c_i^{(r)}),\\
\rho_{O{\rightarrow}S} &=
\frac{1}{|\conf|}\sum_{i\in\conf}c_i^{(r)}(1-c_i^{(s)}).
\end{align}
$S{\rightarrow}O$ denotes street-correct and remote-wrong cases, and
$O{\rightarrow}S$ denotes the reverse.

\paragraph{Leakage control.}
The original hurricane endpoint split allowed overlapping object identifiers
across train and test, because a single panoramic image can be tied to multiple
labeled footprints. Without grouping, near-duplicate views of the same object
can appear on both sides of the split and inflate any model that effectively
memorizes a location, an effect long documented for spatially and
hierarchically structured data~\cite{roberts2017cross}. We therefore re-split
with object-level grouping so that no identifier appears in both partitions. The
grouped-clean endpoint split is our conservative control, and we keep the leaky
split only as a contrast that isolates the effect of leakage on the conflict
gain.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

Images are resized to 224 by 224 pixels and normalized for ImageNet-pretrained
ResNet18 encoders. Each view embedding is projected to 256 dimensions. Models
use AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$ for 10 epochs. Grouped splits use an
80/10/10 train/validation/test design with approximate label stratification, and
the best-validation-F1 checkpoint is evaluated on the held-out test split.
Stability checks use seeds 42, 123, and 456. Cross-view validation F1 is
$0.9689\pm0.0011$ on wildfire and $0.9455\pm0.0063$ on hurricane; street-only
baselines are close but lower ($0.9657\pm0.0001$ and $0.9433\pm0.0012$).

Backbone checks rule out a small-CNN artifact. On cross-view validation,
ResNet18/ResNet50/DINOv2/CLIP obtain
$0.9676$/\allowbreak$0.9693$/\allowbreak$0.9378$/\allowbreak$0.7502$ on wildfire
and $0.9694$/\allowbreak$0.9470$/\allowbreak$0.7975$/\allowbreak$0.6437$ on
hurricane. The supervised CNN features remain strongest, suggesting that generic
large-scale pretraining transfers imperfectly to this domain-specific binary
damage task.

\section{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:cae} is the CAE scorecard. Average F1 alone would make CV look
mostly helpful or tied. Conflict F1 changes the reading. Wildfire shows a clear
positive conflict gain ($\dview=+0.0388$), and CV is the strongest configuration
both overall and on $\conf$.

\paragraph{Leakage flips the hurricane verdict.}
The two hurricane endpoint rows are the same minor-versus-severe task; they
differ only in whether object-level leakage is removed. Under the leaky split,
CV looks helpful on conflicts ($\dview=+0.0805$). After object grouping, the
gain flips to $\dview=-0.0153$ while overall F1 changes little and street-only
slightly edges CV ($0.9016$ versus $0.9008$). The flip isolates spatial leakage,
not view regime, as the source of the original positive endpoint gain---exactly
the kind of boundary that aggregate F1 conceals and CAE surfaces.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.}
The hurricane conflict subsets are small ($|\conf|=32$ and $80$ for the endpoint
settings), so conflict F1 there is estimated with high variance. In our released
bootstrap analyses, cross-view conflict-F1 intervals span roughly $0.19$ in
width on wildfire and exceed $0.35$ on the hurricane subsets, and the
single-view and cross-view intervals overlap on the hurricane settings. We
therefore read the negative hurricane gain as a sign-level boundary signal to
replicate, not a high-precision effect estimate, while the wildfire gain rests
on a much larger subset ($|\conf|=435$).

\paragraph{Directional arbitration.}
Figure~\ref{fig:result-diagnostics} explains why the mixed gains are useful
rather than inconvenient. In wildfire, $S{\rightarrow}O$ conflicts are more
common than $O{\rightarrow}S$ conflicts ($0.271$ versus $0.189$), so
property-centered ground imagery is the stronger arbitrator. In the broader
hurricane split the ordering reverses ($0.109$ versus $0.133$), consistent with
panoramic views that contain more scene context and weaker target alignment. The
endpoint hurricane split is closer to balanced ($0.375$ versus $0.344$),
reinforcing that panoramic regimes are split-sensitive.

\paragraph{Significance and threshold sensitivity.}
A label-independence permutation test on the conflict subset supports the
wildfire finding: with $\tau=0.1$ and $|\conf|=435$, cross-view conflict
accuracy is $0.7885$ and the permutation null is rejected at $p<10^{-4}$. On the
hurricane moderate-plus-severe subset ($|\conf|=128$), cross-view conflict
accuracy is $0.7500$ with $p<10^{-4}$ as well, but the two small endpoint
subsets ($|\conf|=32,80$) are underpowered for a strong significance claim and
we do not make one. Ordering is also stable across thresholds: at
$\tau=\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$, CV remains strongest on wildfire conflict accuracy
($0.8349$, $0.7821$, $0.7538$). At $\tau=0.1$, wildfire conflict accuracy is
$0.7885$ for CV, $0.7356$ for S, and $0.6529$ for R. As $\tau$ increases, only
stronger single-view contradictions remain, so accuracy can drop while the
wildfire ordering persists.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

The key result in Table~\ref{tab:cae} is the negative grouped-clean hurricane
gain: once object-level leakage is controlled, more visual evidence does not
always help. CAE thus exposes a deployment boundary, not only a performance
gain. The conflict subset is a second readout, not a replacement for the test
set: its gate uses only single-view probabilities, so it can be computed before
trusting fusion, and the directional counts identify which view supplies the
missing cue. A fused model can therefore be useful for broad screening yet
unsafe for escalation if it loses to a single view on $\conf$.

Spatial observation regime makes this boundary plausible. In wildfire records
the photographer aims at the labeled structure, so the ground image carries
direct facade evidence; in hurricane records the panoramic image captures a
street segment, and the target can be small, peripheral, or occluded. A frozen
building-segmentation proxy makes this concrete: on conflict images the mean
building-pixel ratio is $0.268$ for wildfire versus $0.015$ for hurricane (about
$17\times$ higher), the center-region ratio is $0.410$ versus $0.027$, and the
building centroid is more central in wildfire ($0.296$ versus $0.397$). The
mechanism is \emph{how each view observes the target}, not ``street versus
satellite.'' The proxy separates the regimes but shows no strong per-sample
correlation with fusion success (Figure~\ref{fig:result-diagnostics}c), so we
read alignment as a regime-level, not per-image, explanation; the tile-level
maps are likewise a descriptive audit and warrant a spatial-autocorrelation
summary (e.g.\ Moran's $I$) before any tile-level claim. Operationally,
agreement cases use the agreed decision while disagreement cases route to CV or
human review, and a negative $\dview$ signals deferring to the dominant view.

Several limitations bound these claims: the two settings differ in hazard,
geography, protocol, and labels, so regime is an explanatory lens, not causal
proof; the alignment proxies are diagnostic, not field-verified geometry; the
fusion model is simple by design; a non-learned aggregation baseline (averaging
or majority vote) should be added; and object grouping matters as much as
architecture~\cite{roberts2017cross}. A stronger benchmark would collect
multiple ground-view regimes for the \emph{same} buildings, possibly with
synthesized cross-view imagery~\cite{yang2026satellite}. In summary, CAE
reframes paired-view triage as conflict resolution and reports, per view and for
the fused model, the tuple $\mathbf{r}_{\tau}(m)$, $\dview$, the conflict count
$|\conf|$, and the $S{\rightarrow}O$ versus $O{\rightarrow}S$ balance. It shows a
positive wildfire gain, a hurricane gain that flips negative once leakage is
removed, and a dominant-view switch tied to regime. Benchmarks should thus
report conflict-subset behavior, directional arbitration, and threshold
sensitivity under grouped splits; a natural next step is conflict-aware training
that reweights $\conf$, pending comparison against standard focal and
class-balanced losses.
