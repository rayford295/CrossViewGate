\begin{abstract}
Cross-view disaster assessment combines ground-level and overhead imagery to
infer building damage. Aggregate F1 scores, however, hide the cases that matter
most for deployment: examples where the single-view predictors disagree. This
study introduces Conflict-Aware Evaluation (CAE), a protocol that reports
overall F1, conflict-subset F1, and the conflict gain of cross-view fusion over
the stronger single-view baseline. We apply CAE to two paired disaster
settings: an Eaton/Altadena wildfire dataset with property-centered ground
imagery and the Hurricane Ian CVDisaster benchmark with panoramic ground
imagery. The results show both the promise and the boundary of fusion. On the
wildfire sensitivity split, cross-view fusion improves conflict F1 by 0.0388
over the best single-view baseline. On the leakage-controlled hurricane
endpoint split, however, the gain becomes negative ($\dview=-0.0153$), even
though aggregate F1 makes cross-view and street-only inference look nearly
equivalent. A directional decomposition explains the switch: wildfire conflicts
more often involve street-correct and overhead-wrong cases, while the broader
hurricane split reverses this pattern. These findings support a simple
evaluation shift: paired-view disaster models should be judged first on the
conflict cases that expose their boundary conditions, not only on average-case
scores.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{disaster assessment, cross-view learning, multimodal fusion, GeoAI, conflict-aware evaluation}

\section{Introduction}

Post-disaster building assessment increasingly uses several visual viewpoints.
Overhead imagery gives broad spatial coverage, while ground imagery can expose
facade condition, debris, and local structural cues. Cross-view models seek to
combine these signals, but prior evaluation often reduces their value to one
aggregate test metric. That view is too coarse for operational disaster triage.
Many buildings are easy in both views. The harder and more consequential cases
are those where the two views provide competing evidence.

This paper studies cross-view fusion as a conflict-resolution problem. We focus
on paired examples that contain a ground image, an overhead patch, and a binary
damage label. Instead of asking only whether paired-view fusion improves
average F1, we ask whether fusion resolves the subset where the two single-view
models disagree. That framing matters for geographic AI because observation
conditions are not interchangeable. A close property inspection photograph and
a panoramic street scene both count as ground imagery, but they encode
different relationships between observer, target building, and surrounding
landscape.

The study sits between several lines of disaster-vision and geospatial
evaluation work. Satellite damage benchmarks such as xBD have made
overhead-only building assessment a standard task \cite{gupta2019creating}, and
later fusion models improve satellite-based damage assessment from multi-scale
visual evidence \cite{xiao2023cross}. Ground and street-view studies show that
close-range imagery can capture damage cues that overhead imagery misses
\cite{zhai2020damage,xue2024post,yang2025hyperlocal}. CVDisaster is the
closest paired-view benchmark for Hurricane Ian \cite{li2025cross}. Cross-view
geolocalization work has also established the broader term for matching ground
and aerial views \cite{workman2015wide}; here the goal is not localization but
damage triage under evidence conflict. CAE is related to selective prediction
and disagreement-aware evaluation \cite{geifman2017selective}, but it does not
claim a new risk-coverage estimator. Its contribution is a spatially grounded
reporting unit for paired disaster views: the subset where the two observation
regimes disagree, the single-view direction of correction, and the gain or loss
from fusion. This complements spatial evaluation concerns about leakage and
split design \cite{roberts2017cross}. Recent generative work also studies how
post-disaster ground views may be synthesized from satellite imagery when field
observations are sparse \cite{yang2026satellite}.

This study makes three contributions. First, it defines Conflict-Aware
Evaluation (CAE), a three-metric protocol that reports full-test performance and
conflict-subset behavior together. Second, it applies CAE to wildfire and
hurricane paired-view triage and treats the hurricane grouped-clean split as the
conservative endpoint control. This control produces the central negative
result: fusion is not always the best conflict resolver after leakage is
removed. Third, it introduces spatial observation regime as the explanation
scale for this boundary. The analysis deliberately avoids a causal claim that
disaster type alone determines which view should dominate. The evidence is
better read as a regime-sensitive experiment: cross-view fusion helps most when
single-view evidence conflicts, and the source of that help depends on how the
ground view observes the target building.

\section{Data and Protocol}

\subsection{Paired-View Triage Settings}

Let
$\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i^{(s)},x_i^{(r)},y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be a paired disaster
triage dataset, where $x_i^{(s)}$ is a street or ground image,
$x_i^{(r)}$ is an overhead patch, and $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ is the binary damage
label. We compare three modes: \emph{street-only} (S), \emph{remote-only} (R),
and \emph{cross-view} (CV). The cross-view model uses late fusion of ResNet18 features
\cite{he2016deep}, including each embedding, their absolute difference, and
their elementwise product. This simple fusion design keeps the evaluation
focused on conflict behavior rather than architectural novelty.

The wildfire setting uses an Eaton/Altadena paired dataset assembled for this
project. Its ground imagery is property-centered inspection imagery paired with
overhead patches. This observation regime often places the target building near
the visual center of the ground image. The hurricane setting uses the Hurricane
Ian CVDisaster benchmark \cite{li2025cross}, which pairs panoramic ground
imagery with very high resolution overhead imagery. This observation regime
often includes wider street context, adjacent buildings, and weaker target
alignment. The comparison therefore mixes disaster type, geography, source, and
collection protocol; those factors limit causal interpretation and motivate the
conservative language used below.

Table~\ref{tab:settings} records the experimental settings and the control role
of each split. This is important because the paper should not choose a split
after observing which one makes the conflict story look strongest. The wildfire
sensitivity split is used because it gives a larger and more informative
disagreement subset. The hurricane grouped-clean endpoint split is included
because it controls object-level leakage in the original endpoint partition.

\subsection{Conflict-Aware Evaluation}

For each example, let $p_i^{(s)}$ and $p_i^{(r)}$ denote the positive-class
probabilities predicted by the street-only and remote-only models. CAE defines
the conflict subset
\begin{equation}
\conf=\{i: |p_i^{(s)}-p_i^{(r)}|>\tau\},
\end{equation}
with $\tau=0.1$ in the main analysis. For each model $m$, CAE reports standard
overall F1 and conflict F1 on $\conf$. It then computes the cross-view conflict
gain
\begin{equation}
\dview =
\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{crossview};\conf)
-
\max\{\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{street};\conf),
\mathrm{F1}(\mathrm{remote};\conf)\}.
\end{equation}
This protocol exposes whether paired-view fusion resolves disagreement cases
rather than only improving easy average cases.

We also decompose conflicts by correction direction. $S{\rightarrow}O$ denotes
cases where the street-only model is correct and the remote-only model is
wrong; $O{\rightarrow}S$ denotes the reverse. These fractions identify the
stronger single-view arbitrator inside the conflict subset. Because a prior
hurricane endpoint split had object-level leakage, we report the grouped-clean
endpoint split as a required control rather than as an optional robustness run.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

All modes use the same triage implementation. Images are resized to 224 by 224
pixels and normalized for the selected backbone. The main runs use ImageNet
pretrained ResNet18 encoders. Each view embedding is projected to 256
dimensions; the cross-view classifier receives the street embedding, overhead
embedding, absolute difference, and elementwise product. Models are optimized
with AdamW using learning rate $10^{-4}$ for 10 epochs, and the checkpoint with
the best validation F1 is evaluated on the held-out test split. This keeps the
comparison between street-only, remote-only, and cross-view models tied to the
same training protocol.

\section{Results}

\subsection{CAE Summary}

Table~\ref{tab:cae} reports the core CAE results. If the analysis only reported
overall F1, the conclusion would be incomplete: cross-view fusion is slightly
better in wildfire, slightly better on two hurricane splits, and nearly tied
with street-only on the grouped-clean hurricane endpoint split. CAE changes the
question. It asks whether fusion helps on the cases where the two single-view
models disagree.

The answer is useful because it is not uniformly positive. The wildfire
sensitivity split has the clearest positive conflict gain: cross-view fusion
reaches 0.6618 conflict F1, which is 0.0388 above the better single-view
baseline. The original hurricane endpoint split also shows a large gain, but
its small conflict subset ($n=32$) and leakage history make it diagnostic
rather than primary evidence. The broader hurricane moderate-plus-severe split
keeps a smaller positive gain. The grouped-clean endpoint split provides the
most important boundary condition: cross-view fusion remains nearly tied with
street-only overall (0.9008 versus 0.9016), but on conflict cases it falls below
street-only by 0.0153. This negative result is not a failure of CAE; it is the
reason CAE is needed. It shows that aggregate scores can hide the point at
which fusion stops being the best conflict resolver.

\subsection{Dominant-View Switch}

The directional decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:dominance} explains why the
conflict gain changes across settings. On wildfire, $S{\rightarrow}O$ conflicts
are more common than $O{\rightarrow}S$ conflicts (0.2713 versus 0.1885), so the
ground image is the stronger single-view arbitrator. This matches the
observation regime: property-centered ground imagery often places the target
building near the image center and exposes facade, debris, and local damage
cues that an overhead patch can miss.

The hurricane settings behave differently. On the endpoint split, the two
directions are near tied (0.3750 versus 0.3438), which helps explain the large
but unstable positive gain. On the broader hurricane split, the ordering
reverses (0.1094 versus 0.1328), making the overhead view the stronger
single-view arbitrator. This does not mean that hurricanes favor overhead
imagery in general. It means that panoramic ground imagery, with wider scene
context and weaker target alignment, can behave differently from
property-centered inspection imagery. The stable finding is the switch itself:
dominant-view behavior changes with spatial observation regime and split
definition.

\subsection{Sensitivity Checks}

The conflict-centered interpretation is not only a threshold artifact. A
separate sweep at $\tau=\{0.1,0.3,0.5\}$ keeps CV strongest on wildfire
conflict accuracy (0.8349, 0.7821, 0.7538) and on the hurricane
moderate-plus-severe conflict subset (0.7097, 0.6400, 0.6190). Under the
reporting threshold $\tau=0.1$, CV accuracy on conflict cases is 0.7885 for the
wildfire sensitivity split, compared with 0.7356 for S and 0.6529 for R. On
the hurricane moderate-plus-severe split, the corresponding values are 0.7500,
0.7031, and 0.7266. Label-independence permutation tests are below $10^{-4}$ in
both settings.

Multi-seed validation supports the base ordering without changing the main
claim. Wildfire CV reaches $0.9689\pm0.0011$ validation F1, while S and R
reach $0.9657\pm0.0001$ and $0.9654\pm0.0015$. Hurricane CV reaches
$0.9455\pm0.0063$, compared with $0.9433\pm0.0012$ and $0.8959\pm0.0065$.
Backbone checks add a practical caution: ResNet18 and ResNet50 remain strong,
while generic CLIP and DINOv2 features underperform the supervised CNN
baselines in this disaster-specific triage setting. These checks support the
evaluation lens, but they are not the primary contribution. The primary
contribution is the positive-and-negative conflict pattern exposed by
Tables~\ref{tab:cae} and~\ref{tab:dominance}.

\section{Discussion}

The experiment supports CAE as a useful evaluation protocol for paired-view
disaster triage. Aggregate F1 alone would make the models look similar in some
settings. CAE reveals how they behave on disagreement cases and shows that
cross-view value can shrink, grow, or become negative relative to the stronger
single-view model after leakage control.

\subsection{Boundary Conditions Are Results}

The grouped-clean hurricane result is the most important stress test in the
paper. It shows that more visual evidence does not automatically produce a
better decision once the evaluation split removes object-level leakage. This is
not a weakness of the study. It is the kind of result that deployment-oriented
GeoAI needs: a condition under which a plausible multimodal assumption stops
holding. In disaster response, the costly mistake is not only using too little
information. It is also trusting a fused model in the subset where a simpler
single-view model is already the better arbitrator.

This framing also clarifies the role of the original hurricane endpoint split.
The endpoint result remains useful for benchmark continuity, but it should not
carry the main conclusion once the grouped-clean split is available. The clean
split sets the conservative boundary; the endpoint split shows why that
boundary is needed. Reporting both prevents the paper from choosing the
partition that makes fusion look strongest.

The same principle applies to the wildfire dataset. Its positive result is
credible only if the dataset is not treated as a black box. The paper artifact
will therefore release wildfire split manifests and a data-card summary with
train/validation/test counts, class balance under the sensitive label mapping,
objectid grouping, image-source fields, coordinate fields, missing-pair
filtering rules, and access notes for the raw imagery. The raw images are not
tracked directly in the GitHub repository, so this metadata release is the
reproducible unit for verifying the split and reconstructing the paired-view
benchmark.

\subsection{Spatial Observation Regime}

The dominant-view switch suggests that \emph{ground imagery} is too broad a category
for paired-view disaster assessment. Property-centered ground imagery and
panoramic street imagery observe target buildings differently. The former often
aligns the camera with the damaged structure and can expose close facade and
debris cues. The latter provides wider street context, adjacent buildings, and
scene-level evidence, but it may weaken the correspondence between the ground
view and the labeled overhead footprint.

Spatial observation regime names this difference. It is not a new sensor type
or model architecture. It is the relationship among observer, target building,
viewpoint geometry, and surrounding context. CAE makes that relationship
measurable because it asks which single view corrects the other when they
conflict. In the wildfire setting, the property-centered view more often
corrects the overhead view. In the broader hurricane setting, the overhead view
more often corrects the panoramic view. The mechanism is therefore not simply
"street versus satellite"; it is how each view observes the target under a
specific collection regime.

This concept should transfer beyond the two datasets used here. Any multimodal
GeoAI task that pairs overhead imagery with ground, mobile, or generated views
faces the same problem: the modalities may agree on easy cases and disagree
where target alignment, occlusion, context, or collection timing matters. CAE
turns those disagreements into a reportable unit rather than averaging them
away.

This wording also avoids a common shortcut in multimodal evaluation. A dataset
description may say that it contains satellite and street-view imagery, but the
operational question is more precise: does the ground view observe the labeled
object, the surrounding block, or only a nearby scene? A benchmark that records
this observation relation can explain why a fused model succeeds in one place
and fails in another. Without that record, a single aggregate score can make two
very different sensing situations look comparable.

\subsection{Implications and Next Steps}

CAE suggests a practical triage policy. If street-only and remote-only
predictions agree with high confidence, a response system can often use the
cheaper agreement decision. If they disagree, the case becomes a candidate for
cross-view fusion or human review. The exact threshold should be tuned for each
deployment, but the principle is stable: cross-view reasoning is most valuable
when the evidence is already in tension.

The same view can guide dataset design. A future benchmark should collect
property-centered and panoramic ground imagery for the same disaster and the
same target buildings. That design would remove many confounders in the present
comparison and would let CAE test whether target alignment, rather than hazard
type or data source, drives the dominant-view switch. It would also support
spatial diagnostics, such as mapping where conflict density clusters across
neighborhoods, but those analyses should be treated as follow-up work rather
than the main contribution of this study.

The main claim is narrower: a paired-view benchmark should first report whether
fusion resolves existing view disagreements.

\section{Conclusion}

This work reframes paired-view disaster triage as conflict-aware evaluation.
Across wildfire and hurricane paired-view settings, average F1 hides the cases
that determine whether fusion is useful in practice. CAE exposes those cases.
It shows a positive wildfire conflict gain, a negative grouped-clean hurricane
gain, and a dominant-view switch that follows the spatial observation regime.
The resulting lesson is practical: disaster benchmarks should report
conflict-subset behavior alongside aggregate scores. Doing so makes multimodal
fusion more falsifiable, more spatially interpretable, and more useful for
deployment decisions.
