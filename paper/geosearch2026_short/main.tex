\begin{abstract}
After a disaster, building damage is often assessed from two kinds of imagery
at once: overhead tiles and ground-level photographs. Most methods fuse the
two views symmetrically, trusting both equally for every building. This paper
looks instead at the samples where that assumption fails. We call them
\emph{conflict cases}: samples on which two independently trained single-view
models disagree. We mine such cases from three paired collections, inspection
photographs from the 2025 Eaton wildfire and street-view panoramas from
Hurricanes Ian and Milton, each matched to very-high-resolution overhead
tiles. Conflicts make up 10--33\% of each collection. On these samples, an
oracle that simply trusts the correct view beats every fusion method we tested
by 0.37--0.41 accuracy, and the gap persists after longer training,
calibration, and backbone changes. We recover part of it with a
visibility-conditioned reliability gate: a linear model that decides which
view to trust from building-visibility features, calibrated per-view
confidences, and the disagreement itself. On the wildfire data the gate is the
only method that significantly beats calibrated probability averaging
($+0.051$ on conflicts, $p=0.0001$) and end-to-end fusion ($+0.072$,
$p<10^{-4}$); on the panoramic datasets it matches them. A controlled
field-of-view experiment explains why: cropping each panorama toward the
building doubles the benefit of fusion, whereas random crops of the same size
do not. Finally, the spatial density of conflicts predicts tile-level damage
without any labels (Spearman $r=0.615$, $p=0.001$). Mining conflicts turns
the question ``does fusion help?'' into ``which view should be trusted, where,
and why?''.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Building-level damage assessment after a disaster increasingly draws on two
large image collections at once. Overhead imagery covers a whole region within
hours and is the basis of standardized damage
mapping~\cite{gupta2019creating}. Ground-level imagery, whether crowdsourced
street-view panoramas or official inspection photographs, shows the facades
and debris that overhead sensors cannot
see~\cite{zhai2020damage,yang2025hyperlocal}. Methods that pair the two views
are used for geolocalization, damage
mapping~\cite{li2025cross,yin2026triple,li2026towards}, and multimodal
arbitration~\cite{yang2026damagearbiter}. Their usual conclusion is
encouraging: fusing the views improves average accuracy over either view
alone.

We argue that average accuracy hides both the real value and the real
limitation of cross-view fusion. For most buildings in a paired collection the
two views agree, and fusion changes little. The interesting records are the
\emph{conflicts}, the samples on which independently trained single-view
models disagree. Conflicts concentrate where one sensor is blind. A facade can
look intact from above and gutted from the street; a roof can be destroyed
while trees hide it from the road. Finding these records in a large
collection, deciding which view to believe for each one, and reading their
spatial pattern is as much a search-and-mining problem as a modeling problem.

We therefore make conflict cases the unit of analysis and ask three
questions. First, how much information do fusion methods leave unused on
conflicts? We measure an \emph{oracle single-view gap}: the margin between an
oracle that trusts whichever view is correct for each sample and the best
available method. Across three disasters this gap is 0.37--0.41 accuracy,
several times larger than any fusion gain. Second, can the gap be recovered,
and how? We find that per-sample view reliability is largely predictable from
whether the ground image actually \emph{sees the target building}. A
\emph{linear} gate over segmentation-derived visibility, calibrated
confidence, and disagreement features recovers part of the gap and can be
read as a one-sentence rule. Third, is the effect causal? Datasets differ in
hazard, geography, sensors, and labels all at once, so we manipulate target
alignment \emph{within} a dataset with a controlled cropping experiment.
A fourth result is directly useful for mining post-disaster collections: the
spatial density of conflicts is an unsupervised damage map that can be
computed before any labels exist (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Code,
protocols, and derived tables are available at
\url{https://github.com/rayford295/CrossViewGate}.

\section{Data and Protocol}
We use three paired street/overhead collections. They span two hazards and,
more importantly, two ground-view \emph{observation regimes}: property-centric
photographs that are aimed at a building, and panoramas that are not.

\textbf{Eaton wildfire (Altadena, CA; property-centric).} Building-level
CAL~FIRE Damage Inspection (DINS) photographs taken after the January 2025
fire, paired by location with post-event VHR overhead tiles. The six-level
DINS scale is mapped to three ordinal classes (no/trace damage,
damaged--repairable, destroyed). The split has 6.5k/1,966/1,984
train/val/test pairs.
\textbf{Hurricane Ian (panoramic).} The CVIAN pairing released with
CVDisaster~\cite{li2025cross}: Mapillary 360$^\circ$ panoramas over Sanibel
Island, 27~cm post-event satellite imagery, and three-level damage-perception
labels, with 3,248/573/300 pairs.
\textbf{Hurricane Milton (panoramic).} A 360$^\circ$ street-view/satellite
pairing over Florida after the October 2024 hurricane, built in the same way;
1,740/307/254 pairs.

\paragraph{Splits and leakage.}
Eaton is split by property and tile, so all photographs of one structure fall
in one partition. For Ian we keep the released split for the headline
numbers, so that they are comparable with CVDisaster. However, an audit
against the official CVIAN position file found that 299 of the 300 test
samples share a Mapillary sequence with the training data. We therefore also
report a repaired protocol with $0.005^\circ$ spatial blocks and a 25~m
buffer (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Method}
\paragraph{Base models and conflict cases.}
For each dataset we train four models under one protocol:
\textit{street\_only} and \textit{remote\_only} (ResNet-18~\cite{he2016deep}
with a linear head), \textit{concat} (both encoders with an MLP head), and
\textit{crossview} (both encoders with a learned interaction head), which
serves as the end-to-end fusion reference. Training uses class-balanced
cross-entropy, 224-pixel inputs, AdamW, up to 15 epochs with early stopping on
validation macro-F1, and five seeds. A sweep over ResNet-50, CLIP, and DINOv2
backbones changed no conclusion. Writing $s(x)$ and $r(x)$ for the two
single-view predictions, the \textbf{conflict subset} is
$\conf=\{x: s(x)\neq r(x)\}$. Conflict rates are 9.6\% on Eaton, 33.2\% on
Ian, and 25.7\% on Milton.

\paragraph{Oracle single-view gap.}
The oracle counts a conflict sample as correct if \emph{either} single view is
correct. For a method $m$ we report its conflict accuracy $\acc_{\conf}(m)$
and its \textbf{oracle-gap closure},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{closure}(m)=
\frac{\acc_{\conf}(m)-\acc_{\conf}(\text{best single})}
     {\acc_{\conf}(\text{oracle})-\acc_{\conf}(\text{best single})}.
\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
The oracle only chooses \emph{which} of two fixed models to trust. It bounds
what arbitration between existing predictors can achieve, not what new models
could.

\paragraph{Calibration.}
Averaging miscalibrated probabilities can win or lose for reasons unrelated to
information content~\cite{guo2017calibration}. We therefore fit one
temperature per view on validation predictions and evaluate every
training-free fusion baseline on calibrated probabilities. Temperature scaling
does not change the argmax, so the conflict subset is unchanged.

\paragraph{Visibility-conditioned reliability gate.}
A frozen SegFormer-B0~\cite{xie2021segformer} trained on ADE20K gives four
building-visibility features per street image: the building pixel ratio, the
same ratio inside a centered crop of half the image size, their difference (a
centering signal), and the normalized distance of the building centroid from
the image center. Seven more features describe the predictions themselves:
calibrated confidence and entropy for each view, the confidence gap, the
Jensen--Shannon divergence between the two calibrated distributions, and a
disagreement flag. From these eleven features the gate outputs softmax weights
over the street, remote, and crossview probabilities. It is fit on the
validation split by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the gated
mixture, with z-scored features. The headline gate is \emph{linear}. A small
MLP matched it throughout, and linear coefficients can be read directly.

\paragraph{Field-of-view intervention.}
Each $1024\times512$ panorama is cropped to a $256\times256$ window with a
90$^\circ$ horizontal field of view on the horizon band. In the
\textbf{building-centered} variant the window is aimed at the circular-mean
azimuth of the SegFormer building mask. In the \textbf{random} variant it is
aimed at a reproducible uniformly random azimuth. The two variants share
size, projection, and information budget; they differ only in whether the
window points at the built structure. We retrain \textit{street\_only} and
\textit{crossview} on each variant with three seeds, reuse the unchanged
remote models, and compare the gain of fusion on conflicts.

\paragraph{Conflict density.}
We aggregate seed-averaged predictions to geographic tiles. For each tile we
compute the hard conflict density (the fraction of samples on which the two
argmax predictions disagree) and, as a control, the mean predictive entropy of
the single-view models. Both are correlated (Spearman) with the tile's mean
ordinal damage.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Rather than reporting ``significant in $k$ of $N$ seeds'', we average each
sample's paired correctness difference over the seeds in which it qualifies,
and test whether the mean difference is zero with a two-sided sign-flip
permutation test (20,000 permutations) and percentile bootstrap 95\%
intervals.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Overall results and the oracle gap.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows three patterns. First, cross-view fusion beats
both single views on conflicts on every dataset, and the pooled tests confirm
it (crossview minus street: Eaton $+0.246$, $p<10^{-4}$; Ian $+0.078$,
$p=0.013$; Milton $+0.141$, $p=0.006$). Second, once the single-view models
are converged and calibrated, simple probability averaging is a strong
baseline. On Eaton it exceeds plain crossview (0.954 vs.\ 0.940), and
end-to-end fusion does not significantly beat it on any dataset (Eaton
conflicts $+0.034$, $p=0.073$). Reports that learned fusion beats simple
aggregation may therefore reflect undertrained or miscalibrated baselines.
Third, the oracle gap is much larger than any of these differences. Knowing
\emph{which existing model to trust} for each sample would add 0.37--0.41
conflict accuracy. Calibration does not explain the gap, since calibrated
averaging closes at most 44\% of it. The unused information concerns
\emph{when} each view is reliable.

\paragraph{The reliability gate.}
On the property-centric dataset the gate behaves as the mechanism predicts
(Figure~\ref{fig:gate}a). It gains $+0.072$ conflict accuracy over end-to-end
fusion (95\% CI $[+0.046,+0.097]$, $p<10^{-4}$), $+0.018$ on the full test set
($p<10^{-4}$), and $+0.051$ over calibrated averaging ($p=0.0001$). Its
closure of 52\% is the only one above one half. On the panoramic datasets the
street views rarely see the target: the mean building pixel ratio is 0.027 on
Ian against 0.119 on Eaton. There the gate has little visibility signal to use
and matches crossview (Ian $+0.005$, $p=0.70$; Milton $-0.015$, $p=0.57$).
This asymmetry is the central finding. Gating pays where ground evidence is
aimed at the target, and it costs nothing where it is not. The gate
coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:gate}b) keep their signs across seeds. Street
entropy has the largest negative weight, while building centering and the
confidence gap are positive. In words, the learned rule is: \emph{trust the
street view when it is confident and is actually looking at the building.}

\paragraph{Field-of-view intervention.}
Building-centered cropping raises the conflict gain on both hurricanes
(Table~\ref{tab:fov}). On Milton the closure doubles, from 0.177 to 0.365,
which is two-thirds of the wildfire level. Random cropping with identical
geometry lowers the gain instead. The building-minus-random contrast is
positive in all six dataset--seed pairs. Note that building-centered cropping
\emph{lowers} street-only accuracy yet \emph{raises} the benefit of fusion.
The gain therefore does not come from a stronger street model; it comes from
the two views attending to the same object, and the random control rules out
cropping artifacts. Target alignment of the street view is the causal variable
behind the regime effect, which supports reading the gate's visibility
features as causal rather than merely correlated.

\paragraph{Conflict density as a spatial damage signal.}
On the wildfire, the conflict density of an overhead tile is a strong
label-free predictor of its damage (Spearman $r=0.615$, $p=0.001$ over 25
tiles; $r=0.60$ on a $0.01^\circ$ grid; Figure~\ref{fig:density}). The
correlation grew as the models converged (from 0.545 to 0.615). The
uncertainty control behaves very differently: it \emph{anti-correlates} with
damage ($r=-0.37$ to $-0.52$), because parcels burned to the ground are easy,
confident classifications. Conflict density is therefore not model
uncertainty under another name. It is the spatial signature of two sensors
disagreeing about the world. Damage on Eaton is spatially autocorrelated
(Moran's $I=0.27$, $p=0.0001$), so part of the tile correlation reflects
spatial structure. On Milton the signal drops below significance once the
models converge ($r=0.289$), because better single-view models disagree less.
The wildfire result is the robust one.

\paragraph{External comparison and split repair.}
On the same CVIAN pairing, CVDisaster's CGCViT~\cite{li2025cross} reports
77.96 overall accuracy with a 20M-parameter encoder under a random split; our
ResNet-18 crossview under the released split reaches $73.5\pm1.5$. Under the
repaired spatial-block split, macro-F1 drops by 0.05--0.10 for every model.
Random or index-based splits thus overstate spatial generalization. The
single-view ranking (street above satellite) is stable, but the size of the
cross-view advantage depends on the protocol.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
The 0.37--0.41 oracle gap measures the opportunity within a restricted choice
between two existing single-view models, and it is far larger than what
architectural work on symmetric fusion has produced. For mining paired
geospatial collections, the productive question is therefore not ``how should
features be fused?'' but ``when should each view be believed?''. The gate
closes half of the gap on property-centric imagery with eleven interpretable
features; the remainder is a quantified target for richer visibility
descriptors and pre-event reference imagery. In practice the gate needs one
frozen segmentation pass per street image, a temperature, a linear fit, and
the output probabilities of existing models. Its rule fits in one sentence,
which matters where automated triage must be auditable. Where ground imagery
is panoramic and rarely aimed at the target, calibrated averaging captures
most of the achievable benefit. Where it is property-centric, as in inspection
programs and insurance photography, gating pays. Because the driver is
alignment rather than hazard, capture policy is itself an intervention: aiming
cameras at structures raises the value of every downstream fusion component.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The property-centric regime rests on one wildfire dataset. Milton is small
(2,301 pairs). The repaired CVIAN test set has six spatial blocks linked by
shared Mapillary sequences, so strong spatial-generalization claims need more
groups. The ADE20K segmenter measures built-structure visibility, not
\emph{the} target building. The density signal needs replication on further
georeferenced disasters.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
On the samples where the two views disagree, symmetric cross-view fusion
leaves 0.37--0.41 accuracy on the table across three real disasters. A linear,
visibility-conditioned gate recovers a significant part of it; a field-of-view
experiment shows that the mechanism, target alignment, is causal; and
spatially aggregated disagreement is a label-free damage map. Mining conflicts
turns ``does fusion help?'' into ``which view should be trusted, where, and
why?''.
