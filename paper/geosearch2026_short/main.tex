\begin{abstract}
After a disaster, building damage is assessed from overhead tiles and
ground-level photographs, and most methods fuse the two views symmetrically,
trusting both equally for every building. This paper focuses on the samples
where that assumption fails: the \emph{conflict cases}, on which two
independently trained single-view models disagree. We mine such cases from
three paired collections (inspection photographs from the 2025 Eaton wildfire
and street-view panoramas from Hurricanes Ian and Milton, each matched to
very-high-resolution overhead tiles), where they make up 10--33\% of the
data. On these samples an oracle that simply trusts the correct view beats
every fusion method we tested by 0.37--0.41 accuracy, and the gap survives
longer training, calibration, and backbone changes. We recover part of it
with a visibility-conditioned reliability gate: a linear model that decides
which view to trust from building-visibility features, calibrated per-view
confidences, and the disagreement itself. On the wildfire data the gate is the
only method that significantly beats calibrated probability averaging
($+0.051$ on conflicts, $p=0.0001$) and end-to-end fusion ($+0.072$,
$p<10^{-4}$); on the panoramic datasets it matches them. A controlled
field-of-view experiment explains why: cropping panoramas toward the building
doubles the benefit of fusion, whereas random crops of the same size do not.
Finally, the spatial density of conflicts predicts tile-level damage without
labels (Spearman $r=0.615$, $p=0.001$). Mining conflicts turns ``does fusion
help?'' into ``which view should be trusted, where, and why?''.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Building-level damage assessment after a disaster increasingly draws on two
large image collections at once. Overhead imagery covers a whole region within
hours and is the basis of standardized damage
mapping~\cite{gupta2019creating}. Ground-level imagery, whether crowdsourced
street-view panoramas or official inspection photographs, shows the facades
and debris that overhead sensors cannot
see~\cite{zhai2020damage,yang2025hyperlocal}. Methods that pair the two views
serve geolocalization and damage
mapping~\cite{li2025cross,yin2026triple,li2026towards}, multimodal
arbitration~\cite{yang2026damagearbiter}, and agentic assessment
pipelines~\cite{yang2026rapid,yang2026rapidmap}. Their usual conclusion is
encouraging: fusion improves average accuracy over either view alone.

We argue that average accuracy hides both the real value and the real
limitation of cross-view fusion. For most buildings the two views agree, and
fusion changes little. The interesting records are the
\emph{conflicts}, the samples on which independently trained single-view
models disagree. Conflicts concentrate where one sensor is blind: a facade can look intact
from above and gutted from the street, or trees can hide a destroyed roof
from the road. Finding these records in a large collection, deciding which
view to believe for each, and reading their spatial pattern is as much a
search-and-mining problem as a modeling problem.

We therefore make conflict cases the unit of analysis and ask three
questions. First, how much information do fusion methods leave unused on
conflicts? We measure an \emph{oracle single-view gap}: the margin between an
oracle that trusts whichever view is correct for each sample and the best
available method. Across three disasters it is 0.37--0.41 accuracy, several times any
fusion gain. Second, can the gap be recovered, and how? Per-sample view
reliability turns out to be largely predictable from whether the ground image
actually \emph{sees the target building}, and a \emph{linear} gate over
visibility, calibrated confidence, and disagreement features recovers part of
the gap while reading as a one-sentence rule. Third, is the effect causal?
Datasets differ in hazard, geography, sensors, and labels at once, so we
manipulate target alignment \emph{within} a dataset with a controlled
cropping experiment. A fourth result is directly useful for mining
post-disaster collections: the spatial density of conflicts is a damage map
that needs no labels (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Code, protocols, and
derived tables: \url{https://github.com/rayford295/CrossViewGate}.

\section{Data and Protocol}
We use three paired street/overhead collections spanning two hazards and,
more importantly, two ground-view \emph{observation regimes}: property-centric
photographs aimed at a building, and panoramas that are not.

\textbf{Eaton wildfire (Altadena, CA; property-centric).} Building-level
CAL~FIRE Damage Inspection (DINS) photographs taken after the January 2025
fire, paired by location with post-event VHR overhead tiles. The six-level
DINS scale is mapped to three ordinal classes (no/trace damage,
damaged--repairable, destroyed). The split has 6.5k/1,966/1,984
train/val/test pairs.
\textbf{Hurricane Ian (panoramic).} The CVIAN pairing released with
CVDisaster~\cite{li2025cross}: Mapillary 360$^\circ$ panoramas over Sanibel
Island, 27~cm post-event satellite imagery, and three-level damage-perception
labels, with 3,248/573/300 pairs.
\textbf{Hurricane Milton (panoramic).} A 360$^\circ$ street-view/satellite
pairing over Florida after the October 2024 hurricane, built in the same way;
1,740/307/254 pairs.

\paragraph{Splits and leakage.}
Eaton is split by property and tile, so all photographs of one structure fall
in one partition. For Ian the headline numbers keep the released split, for comparability
with CVDisaster. However, an audit against the official CVIAN position file
found that 299 of the 300 test samples share a Mapillary sequence with the
training data, so we also evaluate a repaired protocol with $0.005^\circ$
spatial blocks and a 25~m buffer. Under it, macro-F1 drops by 0.05--0.10 for
every model: index-based splits overstate spatial generalization, although
the single-view ranking (street above satellite) is stable.

\section{Method}
\paragraph{Base models and conflict cases.}
For each dataset we train four models under one protocol:
\textit{street\_only} and \textit{remote\_only} (ResNet-18~\cite{he2016deep}
with a linear head), \textit{concat} (both encoders with an MLP head), and
\textit{crossview} (both encoders with a learned interaction head), which
serves as the end-to-end fusion reference. Training uses class-balanced
cross-entropy, 224-pixel inputs, AdamW, early stopping on validation macro-F1,
and five seeds; a ResNet-50/CLIP/DINOv2 sweep changed no conclusion. Writing $s(x)$ and $r(x)$ for the two
single-view predictions, the \textbf{conflict subset} is
$\conf=\{x: s(x)\neq r(x)\}$. Conflict rates are 9.6\% on Eaton, 33.2\% on
Ian, and 25.7\% on Milton.

\paragraph{Oracle single-view gap.}
The oracle counts a conflict sample as correct if \emph{either} single view is
correct. For a method $m$ we report its conflict accuracy $\acc_{\conf}(m)$
and its \textbf{oracle-gap closure},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{closure}(m)=
\frac{\acc_{\conf}(m)-\acc_{\conf}(\text{best single})}
     {\acc_{\conf}(\text{oracle})-\acc_{\conf}(\text{best single})}.
\label{eq:closure}
\end{equation}
The oracle only chooses \emph{which} of two fixed models to trust, so it
bounds arbitration between existing predictors, not new models.

\paragraph{Calibration.}
Averaging miscalibrated probabilities can win or lose for reasons unrelated to
information content~\cite{guo2017calibration}. We therefore fit one
temperature per view on validation predictions and evaluate every
training-free fusion baseline on calibrated probabilities; the argmax, and
hence the conflict subset, is unchanged.

\paragraph{Visibility-conditioned reliability gate.}
A frozen SegFormer-B0~\cite{xie2021segformer} trained on ADE20K gives four
building-visibility features per street image: the building pixel ratio, the
same ratio inside a centered crop of half the image size, their difference (a
centering signal), and the normalized distance of the building centroid from
the image center. Seven more features describe the predictions themselves:
calibrated confidence and entropy for each view, the confidence gap, the
Jensen--Shannon divergence between the two calibrated distributions, and a
disagreement flag. From these eleven z-scored features the gate outputs softmax weights over
the street, remote, and crossview probabilities, and is fit on the validation
split by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the gated mixture. The
headline gate is \emph{linear}: a small MLP matched it throughout, and linear
coefficients can be read directly.

\paragraph{Field-of-view intervention.}
Each $1024\times512$ panorama is cropped to a $256\times256$ window with a
90$^\circ$ horizontal field of view on the horizon band. In the
\textbf{building-centered} variant the window is aimed at the circular-mean
azimuth of the SegFormer building mask. In the \textbf{random} variant it is
aimed at a reproducible uniformly random azimuth. The two variants share size, projection, and information budget and
differ only in whether the window points at the built structure. We retrain
\textit{street\_only} and \textit{crossview} on each variant (three seeds),
reuse the remote models, and compare the gain of fusion on conflicts.

\paragraph{Conflict density.}
We aggregate seed-averaged predictions to geographic tiles and compute per
tile the hard conflict density (fraction of samples on which the two argmax
predictions disagree) and, as a control, the mean single-view predictive
entropy. Both are correlated (Spearman) with the tile's mean ordinal damage.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Overall results and the oracle gap.}
All paired tests below pool each sample's correctness difference over seeds
and use a two-sided sign-flip permutation test (20,000 permutations).
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows three patterns. First, cross-view fusion beats
both single views on conflicts on every dataset (crossview minus street: Eaton $+0.246$, $p<10^{-4}$; Ian $+0.078$,
$p=0.013$; Milton $+0.141$, $p=0.006$). Second, once the single-view models are converged and calibrated, simple
probability averaging is a strong baseline: on Eaton it exceeds plain
crossview (0.954 vs.\ 0.940), and end-to-end fusion does not significantly
beat it anywhere (Eaton conflicts $+0.034$, $p=0.073$). Reports that learned
fusion beats simple aggregation may therefore reflect undertrained or
miscalibrated baselines. Third, the oracle gap is much larger than any of
these differences: knowing \emph{which existing model to trust} for each
sample would add 0.37--0.41 conflict accuracy. Calibration does not explain
it, since calibrated averaging closes at most 44\% of the gap. The unused
information concerns \emph{when} each view is reliable.

\paragraph{The reliability gate.}
On the property-centric dataset the gate behaves as the mechanism predicts
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). It gains $+0.072$ conflict accuracy over end-to-end fusion ($p<10^{-4}$),
$+0.018$ on the full test set ($p<10^{-4}$), and $+0.051$ over calibrated
averaging ($p=0.0001$). Its
closure of 52\% is the only one above one half. On the panoramic datasets the street views rarely see the target (mean
building pixel ratio 0.027 on Ian vs.\ 0.119 on Eaton), so the gate has
little visibility signal to use and matches crossview (Ian $+0.005$,
$p=0.70$; Milton $-0.015$, $p=0.57$). This asymmetry is the central finding:
gating pays where ground evidence is aimed at the target and costs nothing
where it is not. The coefficients (Figure~\ref{fig:gate}) keep their signs
across seeds; street entropy has the largest negative weight, while building
centering and the confidence gap are positive. In words: \emph{trust the
street view when it is confident and actually looking at the building.}

\paragraph{Field-of-view intervention.}
Building-centered cropping raises the conflict gain on both hurricanes
(Table~\ref{tab:fov}). On Milton the closure doubles, from 0.177 to 0.365,
which is two-thirds of the wildfire level. Random cropping with identical geometry lowers the gain instead, and the
building-minus-random contrast is positive in all six dataset--seed pairs.
Building-centered cropping \emph{lowers} street-only accuracy yet
\emph{raises} the benefit of fusion, so the gain comes not from a stronger
street model but from the two views attending to the same object; the random
control rules out cropping artifacts. Target alignment of the street view is therefore the causal variable behind
the regime effect.

\paragraph{Conflict density as a spatial damage signal.}
On the wildfire, the conflict density of an overhead tile is a strong
label-free predictor of its damage (Spearman $r=0.615$, $p=0.001$ over 25
tiles; $r=0.60$ on a $0.01^\circ$ grid; Figure~\ref{fig:density}). The uncertainty control behaves very differently: it \emph{anti-correlates}
with damage ($r=-0.37$ to $-0.52$), because parcels burned to the ground are
easy, confident classifications. Conflict density is therefore not model
uncertainty under another name but the spatial signature of two sensors
disagreeing about the world. Damage on Eaton is spatially autocorrelated (Moran's $I=0.27$), so tile
correlations partly reflect spatial structure. On Milton the signal drops below significance once the
models converge ($r=0.289$), because better single-view models disagree less;
the wildfire result is the robust one.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
The 0.37--0.41 oracle gap measures the opportunity within a restricted choice
between two existing single-view models, and it is far larger than any
fusion gain. For mining paired
geospatial collections, the productive question is therefore not ``how should
features be fused?'' but ``when should each view be believed?''. The gate closes half of the gap on property-centric imagery with eleven
interpretable features; the remainder is a quantified target for richer
visibility descriptors and pre-event reference imagery. In practice the gate adds one frozen segmentation pass per street image and
a linear fit on existing output probabilities, and its rule fits in one
sentence, which matters where automated triage must be auditable. Where
ground imagery is panoramic, calibrated averaging captures most of the
achievable benefit; where it is property-centric, as in inspection programs
and insurance photography, gating pays. Because the driver is alignment rather than hazard, capture policy is
itself an intervention: aiming cameras at structures raises the value of
fusion.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The property-centric regime rests on one wildfire dataset. Milton is small
(2,301 pairs). The repaired CVIAN test set has only six spatial blocks, so strong
spatial-generalization claims need more groups. The ADE20K segmenter measures built-structure visibility, not
\emph{the} target building. The density signal needs replication on further
georeferenced disasters.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Where the two views disagree, symmetric cross-view fusion leaves 0.37--0.41
accuracy on the table on three real disasters. A linear,
visibility-conditioned gate recovers a significant part of it, a
field-of-view experiment shows that target alignment is the causal mechanism,
and spatially aggregated disagreement is a label-free damage map. Mining
conflicts turns ``does fusion help?'' into ``which view should be trusted,
where, and why?''.
